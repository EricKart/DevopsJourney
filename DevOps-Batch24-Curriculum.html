DevOps Batch 24 — Six-Month Curriculum

Bottom–to–top: from “how a computer runs a program” to a FAANG-ready DevOps / SRE / Cloud engineer. This is a project-based, mentor-led course — every session produces real work that lives in your GitHub, and by the end you have one production system you built and can defend in an interview. Classes run Tuesday, Wednesday and Friday, one hour each; the mentor covers a concept, then hands you a task due at the next session that you build on your own time.

27 weeks Tue / Wed / Fri · 1 hr 72 mentored sessions 15 phases Primary cloud: AWS Spine project: snipd Attendance: all 3 / week mandatory
How to read this & how the course works
  • The flow never changes: learn a concept in class → build it yourself before the next session → bring your gaps back to the mentor.
  • Sections Map → The arc are the shape of the six months. Sessions S1–S72 is the day-by-day sheet — one row per class.
  • Every task ends in a committed artifact (script, manifest, Terraform, PR, doc, or diagram) plus one written “what broke and how I fixed it” paragraph. That paragraph becomes interview war-story material.
  • The DSA problem sheet is separate — shared with you shortly, worked on your own time. In class we cover the patterns and the coding-round format, not the volume.
  • All three weekly sessions are compulsory. Low attendance means removal from the training — see the attendance rule.

THE MODEL

How this course runs

Final-year students do not want a tour of commands. They want real projects, real commitment, and proof they can put on a resume. Everything below is built around that.

Why it’s structured this way — 20 sec

One hour, three times a week, is not enough time to learn DevOps by lecture. It is enough to review your last build, unblock you, and hand you the next problem. The learning happens in the hours you put in between classes. The mentor is the multiplier, not the source.

Part 1

In the classroom (Tue / Wed / Fri, 1 hr)

The point: class time is scarce, so it is spent only on the two things that need a room full of people and a mentor.
  • Review the task that was distributed last session — walkthroughs, the common mistakes, the better way.
  • Mentor the new concept — the problem it solves first, then a live demo, and every new term defined in plain language before it is used.
  • The task for the next session is handed out with its rubric and a “done looks like this” example.
Part 2

After the classroom — your responsibility

The point: once a concept has been covered in class, closing the gap is on you. The mentor responds; the mentor does not go looking.
  • The build happens on your own time. Finish the task, and if something in it doesn’t click, come back to the mentor — on the group channel, on Google Meet, or in a Saturday slot when one is offered.
  • Bring specific questions, not “I’m stuck”: show what you tried, the exact command, the exact error.
  • A task you couldn’t finish still comes to the next session — with the part you got working and a clear statement of where it broke.
Think of it like

The mentor is a gym coach, not a personal trainer who lifts the weight for you. You get shown the movement and given the programme. The reps — and the results — are yours.

Part 3

Every task

  • Is due at the start of the next session.
  • Produces a committed artifact in your GitHub (script, manifest, Terraform, PR, doc, or diagram).
  • Is graded Pass / Redo on the 4-point rubric below — a Redo is re-submitted with the next task.
  • Ends with one written “what broke and how I fixed it” paragraph.
Grading rubric — applied to every task
CriterionWhat it means
WorksIt actually runs / deploys / passes.
CommittedIn GitHub, sane commit messages, has a README.
DocumentedCommands + expected output + the “what broke” paragraph.
ExplainedYou can answer “why did you do it this way?” in 60 seconds.
Part 4

How your work is tracked

The point: “it works on my laptop” is not evidence. A commit, a running URL, and a log line are.

Every experiment and every deployment is tracked through your git status / commit history and through live production logs. Your GitHub is your progress report — keep it clean and current. When you claim a task is done, the mentor should be able to see the commits that did it and, for anything deployed, the logs that prove it ran.

Attendance — not negotiable

All three sessions every week (Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday) are compulsory unless the mentor says otherwise. Students with low attendance will be removed from the training. If you genuinely must miss a session, tell the mentor in advance and catch up before the next one — the task is still due on time.

The DSA sheet is separate

A dedicated DSA problem sheet will be shared with you shortly. You are expected to work through it steadily, on your own, alongside the sessions, and to bring blockers to the mentor. In class we cover the patterns and the coding-round format — the volume practice is on the sheet, done by you. Sessions S23 and S24 kick it off and set the working rhythm.

Is this course for you? — answer honestly
You can only attend 2 of the 3 sessions most weeks. Are you a fit?
No. The three-session rhythm is the spine of the course — miss a third of it every week and the staircase breaks. Sort the clash before you start, or wait for a later batch.
You expect the mentor to check in when you go quiet for a week.
Wrong expectation. After a concept is covered, reaching out is your job. The mentor answers fast and well — but you have to ask.
You’d rather “cover” 20 tools than ship one system end to end.
Re-think it. The capstone — one system you can demo and explain — is what gets you hired. Breadth without a working project loses the interview.

The spine project

snipd — small code, real infrastructure needs

snipd is a URL shortener with click analytics: create a short link, redirect, count the hits. The application is FastAPI (Python) with PostgreSQL for links and Redis for the hot-path cache and rate limiting. It is deliberately tiny to write and deliberately demanding to run — it needs a database, a cache, config, secrets, scaling, and observability, which is exactly the surface every later phase operates on.

Where it enters

Phases 0–5 build foundational muscle and a mini portfolio (scripts, a Python CLI, DSA solutions). snipd itself begins at S25 (Docker) and every session after that threads through it — so by Demo Day you have carried one system through containerisation, AWS, Terraform, Kubernetes, CI/CD, observability and security.

Cost discipline

Everything is disposable. Stacks are named b24-<phase>-<name>, torn down at the end of each phase, and rebuilt from your own Terraform/scripts — that repeatability is the point. The billing alarm is your entry ticket: no alarm, no cloud work.
